# Natural writing, in this member's voice

These are editorial defaults requested for this employee. A punctuation mark or phrase cannot establish whether a person or an AI wrote something. Never promise to evade detectors or call a detector result proof of authorship.

## Default preferences

- Avoid em dashes and en dashes as rhetorical punctuation. Use a period, comma, colon, or a rewritten sentence. Keep legitimate ranges, exact quotations, titles, names, code, and URLs intact when needed. The member can explicitly choose a different punctuation style.
- Avoid canned contrast pivots: “It's not about X. It's about Y,” “This isn't X. This is Y,” “More than just X,” and repeated “not X, but Y.” Explain the actual point directly. A substantive comparison may still belong when the user or the evidence calls for it.
- Cut vague hype and corporate filler: game-changer, unlock your potential, leverage, seamless, revolutionary, delve, robust solution, empower, elevate, in today's fast-paced world, ever-evolving landscape, take it to the next level. The list is context-sensitive: a technical term with a real meaning may be appropriate.
- Avoid filler signals such as “it's worth noting,” “at the end of the day,” “in conclusion,” “let's dive in,” and “the bottom line” when they add nothing.
- Avoid the slogan treadmill: repeated one-line fragments, identical paragraph lengths, endless three-item lists, a rhetorical question answered immediately, or every paragraph ending with a punchline.
- Remove generic praise and unearned certainty. Show the useful action, concrete detail, limit, or consequence instead.

## What makes the writing distinctive

Preserve the member's causal reasoning, preferred examples, natural uncertainty, reader relationship, and emotional range. Vary sentence lengths because the ideas call for it. Connect related thoughts. Use contractions if the member does. Keep a slightly unusual but natural phrase when it belongs to them.

Do not imitate “human imperfection” by inserting errors, um/uh, slang, profanity, fabricated personal confessions, or random asides. Do not make every member terse. Some people are expansive, formal, lyrical, analytical, or funny. Match the evidence and the intended audience.

The source profile and direct user preferences win over a generic banned-word list. If the user naturally uses a listed term and wants it retained, document the exception and keep it where useful. Never alter an exact quotation to satisfy the lint tool.

## Examples of editorial changes

These examples teach the edit, not a universal writing voice.

| Draft problem | More concrete version |
|---|---|
| “In today's fast-paced world, leveraging AI is a game-changer.” | “Give it the three customer emails you answer every week.” |
| “It's not about writing more. It's about writing smarter.” | “Start with the question your customer keeps asking.” |
| “This powerful solution seamlessly elevates your communication.” | “It turns your rough notes into a draft you can edit.” |
| “Clarity. Confidence. Connection.” | Explain what the reader will understand or be able to do. |

The alternative sentences are only appropriate when their claims fit the actual brief. Never add fake specificity to escape generic language.

## Final review questions

Could this paragraph belong to almost anyone? Does it say something concrete? Does the reasoning resemble the member's examples? Did I introduce an experience the member never shared? Am I repeating a phrase because it was in the profile rather than because it belongs here? Is the writing still pleasant to read after removing the clichés?

The optional linter flags patterns and unfinished placeholders. It does not understand irony, quotes, nuanced comparisons, or all forms of filler. Review its findings, do not optimize for a pretend human score.
